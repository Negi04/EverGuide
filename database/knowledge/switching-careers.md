## Identify your transferable skills

Most career changers have more relevant experience than they think. Skills such as communication, problem solving, working with customers, organising work, analysing information and managing time transfer across fields. List concrete examples from your current work, for example "handled 40 customer issues a day and identified the three most common causes". Specific examples make it easier to explain your value to a new employer.

## Find the gap between where you are and the target role

Collect ten to fifteen job postings for the role you want and note the skills and tools that appear repeatedly. Mark each one as already strong, partly there, or missing. Focus your learning on the missing skills that appear most often, rather than trying to learn everything.

## Build evidence, not just certificates

Courses help you learn, but employers usually want evidence that you can do the work. Small projects that solve realistic problems, ideally related to your current industry, are often more persuasive than another certificate. For example, someone moving from customer support into data analysis might analyse anonymised support data and present clear findings.

## Use bridge roles

A direct jump is not always possible. Bridge roles combine your current experience with the new field, such as moving from teaching into training design, or from sales into a customer success role at a technology company. Internal moves within your current organisation can also be easier because people already know your work.

## Plan for money and time

Changing careers can involve a period of lower income or extra study. Estimate how long the transition may take, build a savings buffer if you can, and decide in advance which signals will tell you it is working. Keep the plan realistic and review it every few months.
